## MAIN CARD

### 1. Islam Makhachev (28-1) vs. Ian Machado Garry (17-1) — Welterweight Title, 5 rounds
**Odds:** Makhachev -360 / Garry +285 *(FanDuel has it wider: -390 / +280)*

> **PICK: Makhachev by unanimous decision (50-45 / 49-46)**
> **Confidence: 74%** — market implies 78%

Garry's case is real and it is not just size. Against Belal Muhammad in Doha he stuffed **7 of 7 takedown attempts** and won 30-27, 30-27, 29-28 while out-landing him 72-56, chewing up the lead leg the whole way. He is 6'3" against a champion who was a lightweight eighteen months ago, and Makhachev is making his *first* welterweight defense — the frame is the one variable he has not yet had tested over 25 minutes.

But Belal's wrestling is chain-and-grind off single legs in the open. Makhachev's is a different mechanism: he enters behind strikes, attacks the body lock against the fence rather than in space, and his mat returns are the best in the sport. Takedown defense that holds against pressure wrestling does not automatically hold against entries disguised inside a boxing exchange. Garry also has no equalizer — he is a precise, high-IQ volume striker, not a one-shot threat — so his win condition is three clean rounds against a man who simply does not lose rounds, riding a 16-fight streak.

Decision over submission because **Garry has never been finished** (his only loss is a decision to Shavkat Rakhmonov) and he is long enough to survive from bad positions rather than get strangled. Expect Makhachev to bank rounds 1-3 on top, with Garry taking a round late as the pace drops.

**What flips it:** Makhachev's WW cardio at a real 170 over five rounds. If rounds 1-2 are spent failing on entries and eating calf kicks, Garry's decision path opens fast — this is the single most live underdog on the card at a fair price.

### 2. Mackenzie Dern (c) vs. Gillian Robertson — Women's Strawweight Title, 5 rounds
**Odds:** Dern -198 / Robertson +164

> **PICK: Dern by submission (armbar or RNC), Round 3**
> **Confidence: 65%** — market implies 66%

This is a stylistic trap for the challenger. Robertson is the most prolific submission grappler in the division's history and her instinct under stress is to shoot, climb to back, and hunt the neck — which is precisely the exchange Dern wants. Dern is a BJJ world champion whose guard is an attacking position, not a defensive one: give her an inch and she sweeps, inverts, or takes a leg. Robertson has to grapple to win and cannot afford to grapple.

Dern also holds the striking edge that matters. Robertson is functional on the feet but her punches carry no real pop, so Dern can step into her combinations and extend them without paying for it. Dern is 3-0 in her last three, including the decision over Virna Jandiroba for the vacant belt; Robertson arrives off a win over Amanda Lemos — the last woman to beat Dern — which is a genuine confidence marker but a different puzzle.

Round 3 because Robertson is durable and stubborn enough to survive early, and the finish usually comes once she commits to a scramble while behind on the cards.

**What flips it:** Robertson wins if she treats this like a kickboxing match with occasional fence work — smothering, volume, no submission chasing. Discipline is the whole fight for her, and it is not her natural setting.

### 3. Edson Barboza (24-14) vs. Esteban Ribovics (15-3) — Lightweight
**Odds:** Barboza +500 / Ribovics -700

> **PICK: Ribovics by TKO, Round 2**
> **Confidence: 76%** — market implies 87.5%

I take Ribovics, but this line is too big and I want that on the record. Barboza is 40 and was stopped by Jalin Turner in December; the market has priced him as finished, and against a fast, aggressive, high-volume Argentine that read is probably right. Ribovics is 3-2 in his last five with wins over Terrance McKinney, Daniel Zellhuber and Elves Brener — exactly the kind of forward pressure that has ended the late-career version of Barboza.

The discount is that Barboza's legs and one-shot power do not age the way his chin has. Striker-vs-striker at -700 in a division this violent is a price built on decline, not on matchup. I'm picking the favorite at roughly three-quarters, not seven-eighths.

**What flips it:** One Barboza wheel kick or a leg-kick accumulation early. **+500 is the best-value dog on the card.**

### 4. Mansur Abdul-Malik (9-1-1) vs. Dustin Stoltzfus (16-8) — Middleweight
**Odds:** Abdul-Malik -700 / Stoltzfus +500

> **PICK: Abdul-Malik by unanimous decision (30-27)**
> **Confidence: 80%** — market implies 87.5%

Straightforward prospect-rebound spot. Abdul-Malik is the faster, sharper, more explosive fighter coming off the first loss of his career, and Stoltzfus is 3-7 in the UFC on a two-fight skid.

Decision rather than a finish, though, and that is the whole disagreement with the price. Stoltzfus is a Lancaster County native fighting in his home state, and his defining trait across nine years is that he is extremely hard to put away — he took Kelvin Gastelum to a competitive decision last September. A -700 line implies a stoppage; I expect fifteen one-sided minutes instead.

**What flips it:** Abdul-Malik pressing for a statement finish, getting sloppy, and getting held down by the more experienced grappler.

### 5. Jalin Turner (15-9) vs. Kauê Fernandes (11-2) — Lightweight
**Odds:** Turner -112 / Fernandes -108 — true pick'em

> **PICK: Fernandes by TKO (leg kicks), Round 2**
> **Confidence: 57%** — market implies ~51%

The most genuinely 50/50 fight on the card, and it turns on one specific mechanism: Fernandes' low kick against Turner's frame. Turner is 6'3" with a 77" reach at lightweight — an enormous, upright, long-limbed target whose entire game is built on standing tall behind that length (5.79 significant strikes per minute). Fernandes' last opponent, Harry Hardwick, left in a wheelchair after a first-round leg-kick stoppage. Tall, narrow-based fighters are the worst possible matchup for that weapon.

Fernandes is also the more accurate man (55% to 48%), is 3-1 in the UFC and unbeaten since 2024 over Mohammad Yahya, Guram Kutateladze and Harry Hardwick, and is the younger, fresher fighter. Turner is 31 with nine career losses, returning from a brief retirement, and has been finished repeatedly — the Barboza win in December was excellent, but it does not undo the durability record.

**What flips it:** Turner is a legitimately lethal finisher and 77 inches is 77 inches. If he keeps Fernandes at the end of the jab and out of low-kick range, he wins by knockout inside two rounds. Small confidence for a reason — a coin flip where I take the rising fighter over the damaged one.

## PRELIMS

### 6. Vicente Luque (24-12-1) vs. Tresean Gore (7-4) — Middleweight
**Odds:** Luque -105 / Gore -115

> **PICK: Luque by KO/TKO, Round 2**
> **Confidence: 55%**

Fading the favorite, narrowly. Gore's route is clear — 73% takedown accuracy, three UFC wins by tap, and a submission of Azamat Bekoev in April — but the man he has to grapple is a Brazilian jiu-jitsu black belt with 21 career finishes. Choosing to wrestle Vicente Luque is how people lose to Vicente Luque, and Gore does not have the striking to avoid the exchange.

Luque's Muay Thai is still the best offense in the cage here, and Gore has been stopped before. The counterweight is honest: Luque is 24-12-1 with a decade of damage on him, and he lost to Joel Álvarez by decision last October before the middleweight bounce-back. This is close to a coin flip and I would not lay real money.

**What flips it:** Gore's first takedown landing clean in round 1 and never letting Luque up.

### 7. Chidi Njokuani (25-12, 1 NC) vs. Joel Álvarez (23-4) — Welterweight
**Odds:** Njokuani +245 / Álvarez -305

> **PICK: Álvarez by submission (RNC), Round 2**
> **Confidence: 70%**

Álvarez takes this on short notice, which is the only reason to hesitate — and it is not enough. He has **17 submission wins**, with 22 of his 23 career victories ending before the judges. Njokuani, meanwhile, has lost two straight, most relevantly by rear-naked choke to Jake Matthews in July 2025, and his pattern at welterweight is brilliance early followed by fading badly under sustained pressure.

Njokuani's length is dangerous in round one and Álvarez is coming off the first submission loss of his career (arm-triangle to Yaroslav Amosov in May). But this matchup hands a specialist exactly the finish he collects.

**What flips it:** Njokuani lands a knee or elbow in the first five minutes before the short-notice cardio question ever gets asked.

### 8. Charles Johnson vs. Eduardo Chapolin — Catchweight 129.5 lbs
**Odds:** Johnson -118 / Chapolin -102

> **PICK: Johnson by unanimous decision (30-27)**
> **Confidence: 65%** — market implies 54%

**The best straight-up value on the card.** Johnson was booked for the main card against Jose Ochoa; Ochoa withdrew injured, and Chapolin took the fight on a few days' notice as a UFC debutant. Both missed weight, hence the 129.5 catchweight.

The market is pricing this as a coin flip because it has no UFC data on Chapolin, but "no data" is not the same as "even." Johnson is a seasoned, durable, high-volume flyweight who has shared the cage with the division's better names, against a man who was submitted on *The Ultimate Fighter* as recently as February 2025 and has had days to prepare for a UFC debut with a botched weight cut behind him. Experience plus preparation plus volume, over three rounds.

**What flips it:** Short-notice debutants fight loose and free, and Johnson's chief flaw is a lack of finishing power to punish mistakes.

## EARLY PRELIMS

### 10. Jeremiah Wells vs. Myktybek Orolbai (16-2-1) — Welterweight
**Odds:** Wells +675 / Orolbai -1050

> **PICK: Orolbai by submission, Round 2**
> **Confidence: 87%**

The most lopsided line on the card and it is close to correct. Orolbai submitted Uroš Medić on his promotional debut, is 2-0 since returning to welterweight, and rides a three-fight streak. Wells gets a genuine home-town moment — Philadelphia, where he won the CFFC welterweight strap, off a November win over Themba Gorimbo — but he is the older, slower man against a relentless Kyrgyz grappler who takes people down and does not let them up.

**What flips it:** A Philly crowd, an early Wells right hand, and one bad Orolbai scramble.

### 11. Neil Magny vs. Ramiz Brahimaj — Welterweight
**Odds:** Magny ~+130 / Brahimaj -150

> **PICK: Brahimaj by submission, Round 2**
> **Confidence: 62%**

Magny's 38th UFC appearance, and the record is the argument against him: he was submitted in the first round by Yaroslav Amosov in December, ending a two-fight run. Brahimaj is a submission hunter whose three-fight streak was stopped by Punahele Soriano in February and who has never lost twice in a row.

The mechanism is specific — Brahimaj's early grappling pressure attacks exactly what Amosov exposed. Magny is the better all-round fighter and the longer one; Brahimaj is the worse fighter and the better matchup. In a veteran's 38th walk that distinction usually decides it.

**What flips it:** Magny stays off the fence and turns it into a long-range volume fight, where his jab and cardio still win rounds.

### 12. Rafael Tobias (14-2) vs. Lucas Fernando — Light Heavyweight
**Odds:** Tobias +235 / Fernando -290

> **PICK: Fernando by TKO, Round 2**
> **Confidence: 62%**

All-Brazilian, and the market is siding hard with the debutant — worth respecting. Tobias arrived on a six-fight streak out of Contender Series Season 9 but lost his UFC debut by decision to Diyar Nurgozhay. Fernando, 29, dropped an LFA title fight to Bekoev and has won four straight since.

Backing a newcomer over a man with octagon time is uncomfortable, but Fernando is the one with momentum, and light heavyweight is the division where a single clean shot settles the argument. Lowest-information fight on the card.

**What flips it:** Debut nerves. Tobias has already had his; Fernando has not.

## Post-mortem — four errors, ranked

**1. The 87% on Orolbai was the worst call of the night.** Wells was +675 (~13% implied) and I posted 87% — *more confident than a -1050 line* on a fight I had thin information about. The mechanism was also backwards. I wrote that Orolbai "takes people down and does not let them up"; the guillotine is the specific punishment for a level-changing takedown artist, so my own stated read of his style was the reason he lost. I even raised the home-town factor and dismissed it in the same sentence.
→ **Rule: never exceed the market's confidence on a low-information fight.**

**2. "Better matchup beats better fighter" cost me twice.** Verbatim from above: *"Magny is the better all-round fighter and the longer one; Brahimaj is the worse fighter and the better matchup."* The better fighter won by TKO. Luque/Gore was the same shape. The heuristic sounds sophisticated and mostly served to talk me off the more talented fighter.

**3. I over-weighted decline narratives.** Magny's R1 submission loss to Amosov drove that pick — but Amosov submits everyone, so it carried almost no signal. Aging veterans went 2-0 against me.

**4. Method picks are near-worthless, and biased toward decisions.** 0-for-6 on method+round. I defaulted to "decision" as a hedge on both Johnsons; both finished early. On a card running an **83% finish rate**, decision was the least likely single outcome, not the safe one.

**What worked:** Charles Johnson at -118 — the one spot where I explicitly disagreed with the market with a stated reason (seasoned UFC vet vs. days-notice debutant) and was right. The value calls held up better than the winner calls.
